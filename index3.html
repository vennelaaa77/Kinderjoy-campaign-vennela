Fig. 00 — the moment it clicked

The Rakhi gift that started it

what actually happened ✎

My roommate got a Kinder Joy as part of her Rakhi gift. I lit up — "omg, Kinder Joy!" — and went straight for the chocolate. I could not care less about the toy, the chocolate's genuinely good. She looked at me like I was insane: "the gift is the best part."

the actual thought ✎

I said if the toy were something I could use — a charm, stickers, something like that — I'd actually want it too. But every toy is built for a kid. So then it clicked — the concept of market segmentation that I'd learned in class. I was a segment of the market that Kinder Joy simply hadn't targeted yet.

that's the whole project, really — I was already the customer, just never the target.

Fig. 01 — the gap

The customers nobody wrote down

Kinder Joy talks to kids, and the parents who pay for them. That's not the whole customer list — it's just the one that's been written down so far.

who it's FOR ✎

Children 3–12. The parent is the one actually deciding what goes in the cart.

who's REALLY buying ✎

All of the above — plus teens, students, couples, and nostalgic adults. Nobody's written them a single line of copy.

Fig. 03 — who this is for

Two people, two different reasons

Same egg, two different reasons to reach for it — which is why the messaging stays split.

The almost-couple

15–29, giving to someone else

Demographics

  • School, college, or early career
  • Low disposable income, price-sensitive
  • Buying last-minute, around Feb 14

Psychographics

  • Affection shouldn't need to be expensive to count
  • Playful, slightly ironic about romance
  • Gifting is semi-public, often photographed
  • Anxious about saying too much, too soon

The college student

18–24, buying for themselves

Demographics

  • Hostel, PG, or day scholar
  • Allowance or part-time income
  • Buys at odd hours, near campus

Psychographics

  • Splurges here on purpose — this isn't the cheap option, it's the affordable one that still feels like a small luxury
  • Buys it as a reward, not a routine snack — "I did something hard, I get to have this"
  • Peer-driven, not ad-driven, consumption
  • Comfortable broadcasting small wins publicly if the format makes it easy and normal
Fig. 04 — the thought process

How I actually got here

Less about the design, more about the thinking underneath it — written the way I'd actually explain it, not the polished version.

1
starting from a contradiction

I noticed I genuinely love Kinder Joy, but I don't buy it for myself. Instead of shrugging that off as a quirk, I asked why — and the honest answer was that I didn't have an excuse to. I was always looking for one, I just never had it.

↓
2
naming the mechanism

That's when I connected it back to segmentation from class. I wasn't an exception — I was a segment Kinder Joy simply hadn't targeted yet. Once I had language for it, it stopped being a personal observation and became something I could actually build on.

↓
3
testing it, not just trusting it

Before going further, I wanted real numbers behind the idea, not just my own read on it — so I asked for demographic data on who actually buys Kinder Joy.

↓
4
adjusting the method, not the standard

That data doesn't really exist publicly. Rather than guess or inflate confidence in soft sources, I changed how I was building the case — mapping every buyer segment out by hand and ranking them, so the reasoning was structured even without a dataset behind it.

↓
5
facts first, feelings after

Once I had the full list, I deliberately kept it to age, occupation, and buying reason — no psychology yet — so I could pick the two audiences on facts alone. Only after Valentine's couples and college students were locked in did I let myself go deep on the psychology behind each one.

↓
6
reusing my own psychology

The tiered gifting idea didn't come from a brief — it came from realizing that "I need an excuse to buy this for myself" and "someone needs an excuse to confess a feeling" are the same block, just pointed at different people. Once I saw that, the three levels basically wrote themselves.

↓
7
pressure-testing before shipping it

Along the way I kept checking my own logic — catching, for example, that a "safe" tagline could quietly undo its own deniability by naming the hedge out loud. Every line got re-read like that before it was final: not just whether it sounded clever, but whether it actually held up.

Test it on myself first. Then rebuild it for someone else.

Fig. 05 — the concept

Three levels. One egg. No confessions required.

A giver picks how much a gesture means; the packaging does the talking. Every tier keeps the real shell and wordmark — only colour, print, and toy visibility change.

the add-on ✎

Level 03 got heavier without the price following: charm and scratch card, plus a QR on the pack leading to a two-minute "what's your relationship type" quiz — a couple's version of a Spotify Wrapped card, built to screenshot and post. The chocolate stays the entry point; the shareable result is what actually travels.

Fig. 06 — why it works

Why this actually works

costly signalling

People already read the size of a gesture as proof of how much someone means it. The tiers make that instinct explicit.

plausible deniability

No tagline admits intent. "Just because" can always be walked back — the escalation is visible only to whoever picked the tier.

less confession anxiety

For an audience that overthinks a text message, handing over an object does the talking a sentence never could.

a payoff, not 3 products

Level 03's line quietly confirms the first two were never neutral — but only once it's safe to say so.

Fig. 07 — the campaign

Selling it, from the brand's side

Two hashtags, one pop-up format, built for the audience that films everything anyway.

#KinderConfessions

The primary tag — for unboxing a level, reacting to one, or posting the Level 03 quiz result. "Which level did you get?" works as the natural caption on its own, no hashtag literacy required.

#KinderWins

The student-side tag — posted alongside the day's small win, not the relationship. Keeps the two audiences from colliding in one feed.

how #KinderConfessions actually shows up in a feed
the booth ✎ "pick your level"

Pop-ups during Valentine's week, deliberately placed in casual-not-fancy spots — campus food streets, café queues, park benches, the line outside a movie theatre. A two-question quiz on a tablet ("how would you describe them to your friends?") recommends a level on the spot. Walk away with the egg and a small printed confession card, ready to actually hand over — the booth does the hard part of deciding, not the confessing.

Fig. 08 — the walkthrough

One relationship, three eggs

What it actually looks like to live through the tiers, start to finish.

1
the crush

He likes her. Just a crush — nothing said out loud. Wants to gift something but he's scared she'll get weirded out if it reads as too much, too soon.

→ gives her Level 01, "Just because."

↓
if it goes sideways

She's confused, maybe short with him — "why are you giving me this? I don't like you like that."

→ he says, "I just gave it as a friend."

Deniability holds. Nothing lost, nothing said that can't be walked back.

if something sparks

She responds warmly. Maybe she doesn't say anything outright — but something's clearly there now.

The story keeps going.

↓
2
the next step

Some time later, he gives Level 02, "Thought of you. Felt like it." This one's genuinely risky — practically a confession. She should take the hint, even though nothing here is too intense either.

↓
3
certain someone

It's working. He asks her to be his person, and gives her Level 03, "You already know." Not really a question anymore — just a confirmation of something already true.

Kinder Joy just walked you through the whole relationship.

Fig. 09 — the other half

The other customer: students

The original student read was wrong in one place — this isn't bought because it's cheap. It's bought as a reward, on purpose, the way a treat-yourself purchase works.

the insight

"I don't buy it because it's cheap. I want to splurge on it sometimes — it's my reward for doing something good."

"kinder wins" edition

Tiers matched to effort, not romance: Small Win, Big Win, Big Flex. A motivational sticker sits where the kids' toy usually would.

the share loop

QR on the pack opens a share card for today's win, postable to a Kinder community wall — same shareability logic as the couples' quiz, aimed at a different feeling: pride, not romance.

why it's evergreen

No date on the calendar drives this one. Exam season and results day give it a natural seasonal spike, but the mechanic runs year-round — a win is a win any week.
